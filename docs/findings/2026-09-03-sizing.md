## Cores are not the constraint, and that is the headline

At 5,000 sensors the p95 is **208.5 ms on two cores, 212.3 on four, 208.6 on eight** — the three
tiers are indistinguishable, and every one of them is inside `P-15`'s 1,000 ms budget with a
fifth of it spent. Nothing was deferred or refused anywhere in the sweep.

That is not a surprise once stated: a check spends nearly all of its life waiting for a packet,
so a worker holding a slot is holding a socket, not a core. Adding cores buys nothing a
socket-bound workload can spend. **Sizing a probe by core count is sizing it by the wrong
number**, and an operator told to buy eight cores for 5,000 sensors would be buying six of them
for nothing.

## What *is* the constraint: the per-tick burst

The wheel fires per-second buckets, so the work does not arrive smoothly — it arrives in a burst
once a second, and the burst is `sensors ÷ interval`. If the pool cannot absorb a burst in one
round, the remainder waits a whole check for the next slot:

    burst   = sensors / intervalSeconds
    rounds  = ceil(burst / workers)
    jitter ≈ (rounds − 1) × checkMs

Checked against all fifteen points:

| Where | burst | workers | rounds | predicted | measured p95 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ≤ 2,500 sensors, any tier | 2–42 | 4–48 | 1 | 0 ms | 6–18 ms |
| 5,000 sensors, any tier | 83 | 48 | 2 | **200 ms** | **208–212 ms** |

The residual 6–18 ms where the model predicts zero is the wheel's own dispatch cost. Where it
predicts 200 ms it is within 12 ms on every tier. **This is the rule to size by**, and the reason
`docs/sizing.md` is written in terms of it rather than as a table of machines.

Two consequences worth stating plainly:

- **The check duration is the multiplier, not the sensor count.** The same 5,000 sensors with
  2-second checks instead of 200 ms would cost 2 s of jitter per extra round, and two rounds
  would blow the budget outright. A probe watching slow HTTP endpoints needs a bigger pool than
  one watching pings, at the same sensor count.
- **Lengthening the interval helps twice**: it lowers the burst directly, and a lower burst needs
  fewer workers, which is memory as well as latency.

## Memory is the pool, and the pool is its ceiling

RSS tracks worker count and nothing else: 4 workers cost 118 MB, 48 cost about 575 MB, and the
sensor count only matters through the pool size it provokes. Above roughly 2,500 sensors the pool
pins at its ceiling of 48 on every tier.

**It pinned there far below the load that would justify it** — 500 sensors need 1.7 workers by
Little's law and reached 27 — which is what `P-26` turned out to be, and the table above is the
*pre-fix* measurement. Growth doubled while newly spawned workers were not yet ready, so each
tick inside the spawn window bought against a queue it had already paid for; and the shrink
policy timed "nothing has been submitted", which is never true for a minute on a busy probe.

**Re-measured after the fix** (`2026-09-03-sizing-sweep-after-p26.jsonl`, 8 cores, same ceiling):

| Sensors | Burst | Pool before | after | RSS before | after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 1.7/s | 4 | 4 | 120 MB | 117 MB |
| 500 | 8.3/s | 27 | **14** | 343 MB | **216 MB** |
| 1,000 | 16.7/s | 35 | **24** | 431 MB | **312 MB** |
| 2,500 | 41.7/s | 48 | 48 | 565 MB | 547 MB |
| 5,000 | 83.3/s | 48 | 48 | 578 MB | 571 MB |

The pool now tracks the burst instead of the ceiling: −37% resident at 500 sensors, −28% at
1,000. Above 2,500 the burst genuinely exceeds the ceiling, so pinning there is correct.

Dispatch latency is unchanged. The 2,500-sensor point first read 70 ms against 18 before, which
looked like a regression until it was run again: three identical runs gave **10.5, 67.2 and
8.9 ms**, so that point simply has high variance — it sits exactly where a burst needs one round
or two — and one sample of it means nothing.

## These are scheduler numbers, not whole-probe numbers

The benchmark loads a fixture registry, so its RSS is the wheel and the pool and very little
else. A real probe loads the sensor catalogue into **every worker**, and the difference is large:

| | Workers | RSS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| This benchmark, 5,000 sensors | 48 | 578 MB |
| `H-01`'s soak, 5,047 real `port.tcp` sensors over 24 h | 48 | **1,790 MB** |

About 12 MB a worker here against about 35 MB there. **Use the soak's figure for a real probe**;
this sweep is for the shape of the curve, not the absolute footprint.

## The core, and storage

Neither varies with the probe tier, and both are already measured:

- **Core memory**: 310 MB resident at 5,047 sensors, flat across 24 hours at +1.10 MB/h
  (`2026-08-30-soak-24h.md`). The core holds configuration, not checks.
- **Ingestion**: 7,544,624 readings over 24 hours, about 87 a second, with the queue never above
  92 against a warning threshold of 40,000.
- **Storage**: 99.3 bytes a reading including indexes, so 2.8 GB a day at the v1 target and about
  80 GB for the 40 days of raw data PRTG keeps — 6.4 GB with compression
  (`2026-08-25-timeseries-sizing.md`, `2026-08-25-rollup-storage-spike.md`).

## Caveats

- **One architecture.** Everything here is arm64. The shape of the curve is set by check
  concurrency rather than by instruction set, so it should carry, but it is not measured on x86.
- **Cores are constrained, memory is not the thing that fails.** Each tier was given 1 GB a core,
  which is enough that no run came near an OOM. This measures what the scheduler holds, not what
  happens when it cannot.
- **Sleeping checks**, as in `P-15`'s gate and for the same reason. A CPU-burning check would
  make the pool look CPU-bound when it is not — but it also means this does not model a probe
  running `exe.script` sensors that genuinely compute.
- **60 seconds a point.** Long enough for the pool to settle and for the burst to repeat sixty
  times; not a soak. `H-01` is the soak.
- **Nothing here measures a remote probe**, a saturated network, or the core under more than one
  probe's ingestion.
